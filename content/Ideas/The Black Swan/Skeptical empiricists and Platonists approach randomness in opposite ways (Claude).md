There are two ways to approach randomness, and they barely overlap.

The first is skeptical empiricism, which I’ll call the a-Platonic school. It cares about what lies outside the Platonic fold, the things that don’t fit our neat models. It respects people who have the nerve to say “I don’t know.” Its patron saint is Fat Tony. For the skeptical empiricist, Black Swans are the main source of randomness, not a footnote to it. He works from the bottom up. He rarely wears a suit, except to funerals. He would rather be broadly right than precisely wrong. He keeps theory to a minimum and treats the urge to theorize as an illness to be resisted. He does not believe probabilities can be computed easily. His model is Sextus Empiricus and the old school of evidence-based medicine that used as little theory as possible. His intuitions come from practice, and he moves from observation to books, not the other way around. He takes no inspiration from any particular science and is happy to use messy mathematics and computational methods. His ideas rest on skepticism and on the unread books in the library. He starts from the assumption that we live in Extremistan. What he practices is a sophisticated craft, and he aims to be roughly right across a wide range of possible outcomes.

The second is the Platonic approach, and it is the mirror image. It focuses on what lies inside the Platonic fold. Challenge its models and it answers that they are all we have. Its representative is Dr. John. It treats ordinary fluctuations as the main source of randomness and adds large jumps as an afterthought. It works from the top down. Its practitioners wear dark suits and white shirts and speak in a monotone. Everything has to fit some grand, general socioeconomic model and “the rigor of economic theory,” and anything merely descriptive is looked down on. The whole apparatus rests on the assumption that probabilities can be computed. Its model is Laplacian mechanics, in which the world and the economy run like a clock. It relies on scientific papers and moves from books to practice. It takes its inspiration from physics and leans on abstract mathematics. Its ideas rest on belief, on what its people think they already know. It assumes Mediocristan from the start. The result is poor science: an attempt to be perfectly right inside a narrow model built on precise assumptions.
