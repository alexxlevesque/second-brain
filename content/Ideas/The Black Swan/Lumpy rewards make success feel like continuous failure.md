The "Peer Cruelty" section builds toward a portrait of lumpy reward structures and what they cost to live inside. Work tied to a rare, delayed, outsized payoff produces almost no visible output for long stretches of time, and results, when they arrive at all, come all at once rather than in steady increments. That absence gets framed as a kind of continuous failure, not because anything has actually gone wrong, but because the appearance from the outside, and often from the inside, is indistinguishable from failure: no steady income, no recognition, no milestones to report. The passage lingers on how corrosive that appearance becomes, since the hormonal reward system craves confirmation on a regular schedule, and doubt creeps in regardless of whether the underlying work is sound. What makes the lumpiness bearable, in the text, is a demanding capacity to keep deferring gratification indefinitely, with no fixed date attached and no guarantee the payoff ever comes. The section doesn't soften this into a promise of eventual vindication; it treats vindication as merely possible, which is what makes enduring years of apparent waste so difficult. The closing lines locate the real cost in something social rather than purely financial: the accumulated weight of other people's judgment, delivered in small doses, a pause in a sentence, an unspoken comparison, over years of looking like someone who hasn't succeeded. That social toll is treated as the true price of pursuing lumpy, concentrated outcomes, distinct from and harder to bear than the underlying financial risk.
