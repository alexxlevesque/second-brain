Consider what all of them share: in every case, the mind seizes upon what is visible, confirmed, embodied, and tellable, and lets sink beneath it whatever is silent, uncounted, or merely possible. The confirmed fact rises; the disconfirming case that never arrived sinks. The story with a beginning, middle, and end rises; the shapeless truth that resists narration sinks. The risk we have already named and feared rises; the risk we have not yet imagined to fear sinks without our ever knowing it was there. What floats to the surface of our attention is not what matters most — it is what is most easily seen. Call this the cosmetic. And there is a second, related weight pulling in the same direction: our love of the clean category, the labeled discipline, the Procrustean bed into which we insist on trimming reality so that it might be spoken of at dinner parties. Call this the Platonic. Between the cosmetic, which flatters our senses, and the Platonic, which flatters our intellect, the unseen has no advocate. It simply drowns. This is why we do not see Black Swans coming, and why, having failed to see them coming, we then fail to learn the correct lesson from their arrival. We are not stupid; we are simply built to notice the coin that lands, not the countless ways it might have landed otherwise. History hands us only the survivors, the confirmed, the narrated — and calls this the record of what is possible, when it is only the record of what happened to become visible. If there is a discipline to be had against this, it is not cleverness but restraint: a willingness to starve yourself of the cosmetic — the newspaper, the well-told anecdote, the comfortable category — and to go looking, deliberately and with some discomfort, for what has been left out of the story. The watch repairman and the chess player are rewarded for narrowing their focus to what is visible before them; this is the correct method for a bounded problem with rules. But uncertainty is not a bounded problem, and the same narrowing that serves the watchmaker will blind you to the Black Swan. Learn, then, to mistrust the surface — not because what floats there is false, but because it is never the whole of what is true.
