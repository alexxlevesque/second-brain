When people's choices depend on what other people are choosing, there is no reason to expect the sum of those choices to be anything anybody wanted. The audience that fills the back of the hall and leaves the front rows empty didn't plan that pattern; each person simply found a seat that seemed reasonable given where everyone else was sitting. Calling such a pattern an equilibrium tells us only that no one, acting alone, can do better by moving. It doesn't tell us that anyone likes it, or that a quite different and better arrangement isn't also possible. The market is the case we know best, where voluntary exchange tends to reward people for serving one another, but it is a special case. Most of social life has no such machinery, and we should be careful about reading people's motives off the patterns they produce. The pattern may tell us more about how their choices interact than about what any of them intended.
