First, pairs. Many phenomena come in matched sets because every transaction has two sides. Each loan has a borrower and a lender, each sale a buyer, each payment a recipient, and each burglary a victim. When both sides can be clearly defined and counted, they have to balance. If they matter for different reasons, that balance can produce surprising results.
Second, conservation in closed systems. Some quantities are moved around rather than destroyed. Money changes hands without disappearing, people who leave one city show up in another, and pollutants move through an ecosystem. Waste dumped somewhere else still exists.
Third, semi-closed systems. Some populations flow through structures that follow strict bookkeeping. The simplest is a turnstile, where the people waiting equal everyone who arrived minus everyone who has passed through. More complicated versions include chains of bottlenecks, reservoirs that people enter and leave, alternating or circulating systems, and one-way pipelines with attrition, such as age or rank profiles. The most complex involve multiple routes and loops, like the criminal justice system, or free movement inside a boundary that is open in only a few places. Taken together, these can be described as a transition matrix.
Fourth, complementary populations. Some relationships link two distinct groups. Marriage pairs one-to-one, sibling ties link many-to-many, and people are matched with the houses or cars they own. Any change on one side constrains the other.
Fifth, rates of change. Sometimes one variable of interest is simply the growth rate of another. The rise in an immune population equals new cases minus deaths, just as new cars equal the annual gross increase in the total number of cars.
Sixth, aggregates of individual choices. The conditions people react to are often just the sum of everyone’s reactions. Whether I drive depends on traffic, whether I give depends on what others give, and whether I go to the beach depends on how crowded it is. Together, we create the very conditions each of us is responding to, whether or not we realize we are part of the totality.
Seventh, averages that feed back. In a related case, people aim relative to an average their own behaviour helps set. Examples include tipping a little above the norm, grading a little below it, or arriving early for parking or late to avoid waiting. Everyone’s adjustments move the average itself.
Eighth, shared components. Two variables can contain the same element. In national accounts, sales between firms equal purchases between firms. That means the gap between total sales and total purchases equals sales to final users minus payments to people outside the firm sector.
