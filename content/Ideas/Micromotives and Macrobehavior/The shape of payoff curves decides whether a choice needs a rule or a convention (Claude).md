Many choices are simply yes or no: wear a helmet or not, carry a weapon or not, adopt daylight saving or not. And what each choice is worth depends on how many others make it. In a multi-person prisoner's dilemma, each person has a choice that is better for them whatever others do, yet if everyone made the other choice, all would be better off. The hockey player skates bareheaded for the small edge it gives him, then votes for a league rule requiring helmets. Here freedom of choice produces an outcome nobody wants, and the remedy is often to take the choice away. Picture it by plotting, against the number choosing Right, the payoff to someone choosing Left and to someone choosing Right; people drift toward whichever is higher. In the pure dilemma both lines rise but Left stays on top, so everyone ends up choosing Left: one equilibrium, and a bad one. The point k, where the Right line reaches that all-Left payoff, is the smallest group that gains by choosing Right on its own. If k is small, a voluntary club can work; if it is close to everyone, only a binding rule will. Real effects are rarely constant, so the lines often bend, and bending lets them cross. If Right crosses from below, there are two stable equilibria, everyone one way or everyone the other, with a tipping point between them. That is a coordination problem, like daylight saving or which side of the road to drive on, solved by convention rather than enforcement. If Right crosses from above, people settle into a stable mix, as on a road that slows the more people use it. Curves that cross twice give alternating stable and unstable equilibria, so where a population starts decides where it ends. Even a stable equilibrium may not be the best arrangement: the collective total can peak where some take the worse choice for others' benefit, which raises who should, and how they're compensated. Rules, conventions, coalitions and compensation each fit a different shape.
