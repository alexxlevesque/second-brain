Segregation can be imposed by law or organisation, it can follow from differences in income, or it can come out of individual choices, each person responding to the mix of people around them. That last process is the most surprising. Suppose each person is content to be in the minority, provided they're not too heavily outnumbered, and moves only when that modest condition fails. The result, again and again, is a pattern more sharply divided than anyone's preferences required. One person's move changes the neighbourhood for the people around them, prompting further moves, until the clusters harden. A mixed neighbourhood can be stable at some proportions and fragile at others: once one group's share crosses a threshold, departures feed on themselves and the area tips entirely. So a highly segregated outcome is not, by itself, evidence of strong prejudice. And keeping integration may take deliberate effort even where most people would happily live with it.
