I have spent this entire book telling you to be suspicious of randomness, so allow me a confession: I would not want to live without it. Those who try to optimise every corner of their lives, scheduling every hour, rationalising every pleasure, eliminating every surprise, end up brittle. The smallest deviation from plan unsettles them, because they have left themselves no slack. A dose of uncertainty gives you a buffer, a threshold of tolerance for the unexpected, and it is precisely the unexpected that makes life worth living. A pleasure that arrives on schedule is soon taken for granted, while one that comes by surprise is felt. Randomness also does work that reason cannot: Buridan’s donkey, perfectly balanced between two bales of hay, starves for lack of a reason to choose, and a small random nudge would have saved him. The ancients understood this when they drew lots. So the point is not to banish randomness, but to stop being fooled by it. Protect yourself from its ugly side, and leave enough room in your life for its graceful side to find you.
