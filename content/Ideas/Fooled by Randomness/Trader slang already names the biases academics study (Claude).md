Traders have their own names for many of the biases behavioural scientists study. A trader who says “I’m as good as my last trade” is describing what academics call prospect theory: we judge outcomes as gains or losses against a reference point that keeps resetting, not by their absolute size. When a desk talks about the “sound-bite effect,” or advises you to “fade the fears,” it is describing the affect heuristic, also called risk-as-feeling theory. People react strongly to risks that are vivid and concrete, and they tend to discount risks that are abstract, even when the abstract ones matter more.

The trader’s “It was so obvious,” or the familiar jab about the “Monday morning quarterback,” is hindsight bias: once we know how things turned out, events seem far more predictable than they were. “You were wrong,” said after one bad call, reflects what researchers call belief in the law of small numbers. This is an inductive fallacy, where a handful of observations gets treated as proof of a general rule.

Traders also tell “Brooklyn smarts” apart from “MIT intelligence.” Psychologists describe the same split as two systems of reasoning: the fast, practical mind that handles the work in the moment is not the same as the slower mind that reasons things through. Finally, there is the trader who insists, “It will never go there.” Scientists call this overconfidence: taking on risk because you underestimate the odds of being wrong.
